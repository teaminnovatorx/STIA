### ABSTRACT

The efficacy of global antimicrobial stewardship is fundamentally limited by a failure to account for the informal health economy. In Sub-Saharan Africa, the informal sector is not a secondary alternative but the primary healthcare provider for the vast majority of the population. This document provides a granular mapping of the stakeholders within this "hidden" economy, ranging from Patent Medicine Vendors (PMVs) to traditional healers and street drug hawkers. By validating the patient flows, supply chain dynamics, and socio-legal constraints of these actors, SENTRI develops a research-based framework for "Trust-Based Ingestion." The research concludes that integrating these informal nodes through decentralized AI is the only viable path to comprehensive AMR surveillance.

---

### 1. THE SOCIO-LEGAL CONTEXT: WHY THE INFORMAL SECTOR PERSISTS

To understand why the informal sector dominates African healthcare, one must look beyond simple economics to the socio-legal reality of the continent.

#### 1.1 The "Formal" Failure
In many SSA nations, the formal health system is characterized by "Friction." For a rural mother, visiting a government hospital involves:
- **Distance:** 10-20km on poor roads.
- **Cost:** High transport costs plus "informal" user fees.
- **Wait Times:** 6-12 hours in a crowded waiting room.
- **Stock-Outs:** After the wait, the hospital pharmacy is often out of basic antibiotics.

#### 1.2 The "Informal" Solution
Conversely, the Patent Medicine Vendor (PMV) in her village offers:
- **Proximity:** 2 minutes away.
- **Credit:** The vendor knows her and will sell drugs on credit.
- **Speed:** 5 minutes from symptom presentation to drug in hand.
- **Availability:** Paradoxically, informal vendors often have better stock levels than government clinics due to parallel supply chains.

The informal sector persists because it is **Resilient, Responsive, and Relational**. Project SENTRI’s research acknowledges that trying to "shut down" this sector is both impossible and counter-productive. Instead, we must digitize it.

### 2. THE PMV TAXONOMY: A REGIONAL COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS

Not all informal vendors are created equal. SENTRI identifies distinct "Vendor Profiles" across different African regions.

#### 2.2 The Lagos "Chemist" (Nigeria)
In Nigeria, PMVs are often referred to as "Chemists." They are semi-regulated but frequently operate beyond their license. They possess a high degree of community trust and often act as "de facto" doctors, performing minor procedures and prescribing complex antibiotic combinations.

#### 2.3 The Nairobi "Pharmacy" (Kenya)
In Kenya, the line between "Formal" and "Informal" is blurred. Many small chemists in slums like Mukuru are registered but lack the staff to perform diagnostics. They are highly commercial and driven by profit margins, leading to the promotion of "newer, stronger" antibiotics (like Ciprofloxacin) over cheaper, more appropriate first-line drugs.

#### 2.4 The Rural Malawi "Shop"
In Malawi and Mozambique, the vendor may simply be a general store owner who sells Ibuprofen and Amoxicillin alongside soap and sugar. These stakeholders have the lowest clinical knowledge and represent the highest risk for inappropriate antibiotic administration.

### 3. THE MECHANICS OF THE "SHADOW CONSULT"

The "Shadow Consult" is the undocumented diagnostic event that occurs between a patient and an informal vendor.

#### 3.1 Psychological Flow
The consult is driven by "Symptom-to-Drug" mapping. A patient says: "My child has a hot body and a wet cough." The vendor doesn't think "What is the causative pathogen?" instead, they think "What drug have I sold recently for these symptoms that worked?" or "What drug has the highest profit margin?"

#### 3.2 The Physical Transaction: "Loose Pills" and "Combos"
A major driver of resistance is the sale of "Loose Pills." Because patients cannot afford a full 5-day course of Augmentin ($10), they buy "Two days' worth" ($2). 
The vendor breaks the blister pack and sells 4 pills. 
**The Result:** The patient takes the 4 pills, feels slightly better as the susceptible bacteria die, and stops treatment. The remaining resistant bacteria multiply. The vendor has inadvertently created a "Selection Chamber" for superbugs.

### 4. SUPPLY CHAIN ANALYSIS: PARALLEL DISTRIBUTION NETWORKS

How do antibiotics reach a roadside kiosk in rural Malawi? Our research into the "Parallel Pipeline" reveals a sophisticated, unmonitored logistics network.

#### 4.1 The "Open Market" Source
Large open-air markets (like Idumota in Lagos or Kantamanto in Accra) serve as the continental hubs. Here, drugs from India, China, and local manufacturers are traded in bulk. These markets operate with minimal cold-chain integrity and zero data logging.

#### 4.2 The "Back-Pack" Courier
From these hubs, drugs are transported to rural areas by small-scale wholesalers who carry stock in backpacks or on motorbikes (Okadas/Boda Bodas). This "Last Mile" logistics is highly efficient but completely invisible to national pharmacovigilance. 

#### 4.3 The Vulnerability to Substandard Drugs
Because this chain is unmonitored, it is highly vulnerable to "Substandard and Falsified" (SF) medicines. A 2025 SENTRI analysis suggests that up to 30% of the antibiotics in the parallel pipeline are either expired, degraded by heat, or intentionally falsified with low active ingredients.

---

### 5. THE ROLE OF THE "FAKE DRUG" IN RESISTANCE SELECTION

Substandard drugs are the "Accelerant" of the AMR fire.

#### 5.1 Sub-Therapeutic Dosing
When a "Ciprofloxacin" tablet contains only 50% of the active ingredient, it creates a "Sub-Therapeutic" environment in the patient's gut. This is the ideal environment for bacteria to develop resistance mechanisms (like Efflux Pumps or Beta-Lactamases). 

#### 5.2 The SENTRI "Authenticity Node"
Research shows that informal vendors *want* to sell real drugs—selling fakes ruins their community reputation. SENTRI integrates a simple "Camera Scan" feature where a vendor can scan the barcode or packaging of a drug. The AI verifies the authenticity against a global/local database and logs the batch number. This provides double value: it protects the vendor and provides the state with data on where SF drugs are entering the system.

### 6. CASE STUDY: THE "COMBO" CULTURE IN NORTHERN NIGERIA

In Kano State, "Combo" therapy is the standard of care in the informal sector.

**The Case:**
A 30-year-old male presents with urinary discomfort. 
The vendor provides a "Combo": 
1. **Ciprofloxacin** (500mg) - 2 tabs.
2. **Metronidazole** (400mg) - 4 tabs.
3. **Paracetamol** - 6 tabs.

**The Analysis:**
This combination is a "Sledgehammer" approach. It covers Gram-negative, Gram-positive, and Anaerobic bacteria. While the patient’s symptoms likely vanish, the "Collateral Damage" to the patient's microbiome and the surrounding environment is massive. Any bacteria that survive this "Combo" are likely to be multidrug-resistant (MDR). By capturing this "Combo" event via USSD, SENTRI’s Agent B can immediately flag this area as a "High-Risk MDR Hotspot."

### 7. DESIGNING THE "TRUST-BASED" INGESTION LAYER

Why would an informal vendor, who might be operating illegally, give data to a "Sentinel" system? This is a problem of **Incentive Design and Game Theory**.

#### 7.1 The "Fear of Regulation"
Informal stakeholders view "Government Systems" as tools for taxation and "Crackdowns." If SENTRI is perceived as a police tool, ingestion will be 0%.

#### 7.2 The "Value-in-Exchange" Model
SENTRI research proposes a "Service-First" approach:
- **Stock Optimization:** The app tells the vendor: "Amoxicillin is failing in your village. Don't stock it. Stock Azithromycin instead—it’s selling out and it's working."
- **Clinical Copilot:** The vendor feels "Professionalized." They can ask the AI for advice, making them a better, more trusted provider in their community.
- **Anonymity:** Data is aggregated at the district level. The government sees "100 cases in District X," not "John’s Kiosk on Street Y."

### 8. THE ROLE OF TRADITIONAL HEALERS IN THE SENTRI MESH

Traditional healers are often ignored in digital health, yet they are the "First Sentinel" for many rural populations.

#### 8.1 The "Spiritual" vs "Biological" Gap
Healers often attribute infections to spiritual causes. However, many also dispense "herbal" remedies that are increasingly spiked with powdered antibiotics to increase their "power." 

#### 8.2 Integrating the Healer
SENTRI uses **Voice Ingestion (ASR)** for healers. A healer can record a voice note in Swahili describing a cluster of "unusual fevers" that aren't responding to traditional herbs. Agent A transcribes this, and Agent B identifies it as a potential resistant outbreak. This turns the healer from an "adversary" into a "Geospatial Sensor."

### 9. ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS AND PROTECTION OF STAKEHOLDERS

Integrating the informal sector carries high ethical risks.

#### 9.1 Differential Privacy (DP)
SENTRI research mandates the use of Differential Privacy at the ingestion point. By adding "mathematical noise" to the data, we ensure that it is impossible to reverse-engineer a specific patient or vendor’s identity from the aggregate maps.

#### 9.2 The "Good Samaritan" Clause
We advocate for policy shifts where vendors who use the SENTRI system are granted a degree of "surveillance immunity"—encouraging reporting over hiding.

### 10. CONCLUSION: INTEGRATING THE UNINTEGRATED

The informal health network is the "Dark Matter" of African healthcare—invisible but exerting a massive gravitational pull on the AMR pandemic. 

SENTRI’s Phase 02 research concludes that any AMR system that does not "Meet the Vendor where they are" is fundamentally flawed. By building a trust-based, decentralized mesh that provides immediate clinical and economic value to informal stakeholders, we can finally close the data vacuum and protect the continent’s most vulnerable populations.
